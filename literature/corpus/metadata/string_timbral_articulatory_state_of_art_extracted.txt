State of the Art on String Timbral and
Articulatory Techniques: Harmonics, Sul Tasto, 
Sul Ponticello, and Con Sordino
Executive summary
Four principal conclusions emerge from the literature.
First, the techniques considered here do not produce invariant timbres. Harmonics operate primarily by
changing the vibrational regime of the string through nodal constraint; sul tasto and sul ponticello alter
the excitation point and the range of viable bowing parameters; and the mute modifies the mechanical
transmission between string, bridge, and body. Their acoustical effects are therefore conditioned by
instrument, string, stopped length, register , dynamic, bow force, bow velocity, articulation, and room or
recording conditions. Descriptions such as “glassy,” “veiled,” “metallic,” or “ethereal” identify recurrent
perceptual tendencies but do not constitute stable acoustic definitions.
Second, the conventional account according to which bow position alone controls spectral brightness is
incomplete. Bow–bridge distance interacts with bow force and bow velocity within a bounded region of
stable Helmholtz motion. In controlled violin experiments, bow force was found to be the dominant
predictor of spectral centroid, while bow–bridge distance and velocity constrained the playable force
range. Extreme sul ponticello can additionally produce multiple slips during each nominal string period,
reinforcing selected high partials and introducing irregular or noise-like components. Sul tasto generally
reduces the relative energy of upper partials, but its result is likewise dependent on coordinated
reductions or adjustments of force and velocity (Askenfelt, 1988; Guettler , 2002; Schoonderwaldt, 2009). 
Third, instrument-specific differences are substantial. The longer strings of the cello and double bass
increase the physical separation between harmonic nodes and can favour controllable multiphonics. At
the same time, their larger left-hand distances restrict artificial-harmonic fingerings in low positions.
Natural harmonics respond particularly well on cello and double bass, whereas artificial harmonics on
double bass are concentrated principally in thumb position and on the upper strings. The shorter
strings of violin and viola facilitate rapid artificial-harmonic passagework but make small errors in node
placement proportionally more consequential for multiphonics (Fallowfield, 2020; Guettler & Thelin,
2012; Vienna Symphonic Library, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). 
Fourth, the acoustical literature is unevenly distributed. Violin bowing parameters and violin mutes have
been investigated experimentally, and cello and double-bass multiphonics have received some
systematic attention. Equivalent controlled studies of viola, cello, and double-bass mutes, cross-
instrument sul tasto–ordinario–sul ponticello continua, and ensemble-level textural effects remain scarce.
The analytical literature on texture consequently relies mainly on general theories of timbre, auditory
grouping, orchestral blend, stratification, and spectromorphology rather than on technique-specific
perceptual experiments (Goodchild & McAdams, 2021; McAdams et al., 2022; Smalley, 1997).

1. Harmonics
1.1 Acoustics and timbre
Natural and artificial harmonics
A natural harmonic is obtained by touching an open string lightly at a nodal point. The touch inhibits
modes for which that point would undergo substantial displacement and permits modes possessing a
node at, or sufficiently close to, the contact point. A touch at one half of the string favours the second
partial; a touch at one third favours the third partial; and analogous integer divisions select higher
members of the harmonic series. The string fundamental is not merely “made quieter”: the vibrational
regime is reorganized so that a higher mode becomes the lowest strongly sustained component.
An artificial harmonic applies the same principle to a stopped string. One finger first creates a new
effective string length; another lightly touches a node of the harmonic series belonging to that stopped
fundamental. The conventional perfect-fourth configuration selects the fourth partial and therefore
sounds two octaves above the stopped pitch. Major-third, minor-third, and perfect-fifth configurations
are physically possible, producing respectively the fifth, sixth, and third partials, but are less uniformly
practical because they require more exact node placement, weaker high-order excitation, or wider hand
extensions. The perfect fourth has consequently become the standard artificial-harmonic configuration
in orchestral and solo writing (ConductIT, n.d.; Vienna Symphonic Library, n.d.-c). 
The audible result is not a body-independent sinusoid. The selected string partials are filtered by the
bridge and body response, and their relative radiation varies with frequency. The strength, stability,
onset, and noise component of the harmonic are also controlled through bow position, force, and
velocity. Higher harmonics generally require a sounding point nearer the bridge because the relevant
vibration segments are shorter and because a more favourable excitation geometry must be
maintained. Bowing too far toward the fingerboard may prevent a high harmonic from speaking clearly;
excessive force may restore competing components or produce a crushed attack (Lloyd, n.d.;
Schoonderwaldt, 2009). 
The commonplace assertion that harmonics are always weak requires qualification. Natural harmonics
can resonate strongly when the selected partial coincides with favourable bridge and body resonances,
especially on the cello and double bass. Nevertheless, their attack is normally less immediate than that
of a firmly stopped note, and high-order harmonics have a narrower margin of successful finger and
bow placement. Orchestration treatises therefore tend to warn against covering harmonics with dense
or highly projected material. Rimsky-Korsakov’s characterization of harmonics as transparent, brilliant,
and relatively lacking in carrying power records an influential late-Romantic orchestral judgement, but it
should not be treated as an acoustically universal law (Rimsky-Korsakov, 1912/1922). 
Harmonic glissandi
In a natural-harmonic glissando, a lightly touching finger is moved continuously along an open string.
As it intersects zones capable of sustaining different modes, successive or partially overlapping
members of the open-string harmonic series emerge. The sounding result is therefore not a continuous
pitch glissando in the ordinary sense. It consists of a discontinuous, often unstable traversal through
harmonic nodes, interspersed with noise and transitional components.
An artificial-harmonic glissando normally maintains an approximately constant interval—most
commonly a perfect fourth—between the stopping finger and the touching finger while both are

displaced. This produces a more continuously directed pitch trajectory, nominally two octaves above the
stopped fundamental. Intonation and timbral continuity depend on preserving the proportional
relationship between the two fingers as their physical separation contracts in higher positions. Natural-
harmonic glissandi are generally more indeterminate and spectrally discontinuous; artificial-harmonic
glissandi permit stronger directional control but impose greater technical demands (Messina, 2009;
Timbre and Orchestration Resource, n.d.). 
Half harmonics and multiphonics
“Half harmonic” is not a single, acoustically standardized category. In contemporary notation it may
indicate intermediate finger pressure between a fully stopped note and a lightly touched harmonic, a
deliberately impure harmonic with residual fundamental and noise, or a rapid alternation between
ordinary and harmonic contact. These procedures can produce unstable mixtures of pitched and noisy
components, but their outcomes vary strongly with string, finger compliance, bowing conditions, and
instrument response. A verbal definition or notation key should therefore be supplied; a special
notehead alone is insufficient.
Bowed-string multiphonics are more precisely defined as a single bowed excitation producing two or
more perceptually distinct harmonic pitches from one touched string. They differ both from ordinary
harmonics, in which one partial or pitch identity predominates, and from notated double stops, which
use two strings. Guettler and Thelin identified two principal configurations according to the ordering of
bow and finger positions. Carefully chosen rational relationships among bow position, finger position,
and string length produce clusters of reinforced partials. The radiated aggregate is then reshaped by
the instrument body. Their experiments found the procedure most applicable to double bass and cello
and less practical on shorter strings (Guettler & Thelin, 2012). 
Fallowfield subsequently demonstrated that cello multiphonics should not be treated as isolated
curiosities. Their constituent pitch sets, mutation points, thresholds of perceptual separation, and
sensitivity to string length can be catalogued, although performer , string, setup, and bowing variability
remain significant. The literature is substantially less developed for violin and viola, despite recent
performer-led cataloguing projects (Fallowfield, 2020). 
1.2 Notation and performance practice
Three principal systems coexist for natural harmonics:
A normal notehead with a small circle may identify the sounding pitch or , in some traditions, the
pitch that would be fingered normally.
A diamond notehead may identify the touched node.
The touched pitch and the sounding pitch may both be shown, the latter normally as a smaller ,
parenthesized, or stemless notehead.
These systems are not semantically interchangeable. A diamond alone normally indicates finger
location, whereas a normal notehead with a harmonic circle often indicates the intended sounding
pitch. Because multiple nodes may produce the same partial, sounding-pitch notation grants fingering
discretion; touch-pitch notation specifies the physical solution. The string should be identified whenever
more than one interpretation is possible (ConductIT, n.d.; Vienna Symphonic Library, n.d.-c). 
Artificial harmonics are conventionally written as a stopped pitch with a normal notehead and a
touched pitch with a diamond notehead on the same stem. The resulting pitch may additionally be
supplied above. This fuller notation is advisable in complex contemporary textures because it

Harmonics combine effectively with bow tremolo, measured repetition, glissando, trills between
stopped and harmonic contact, and certain double stops. Their combination with extreme sul tasto is
less reliable because the high-frequency excitation needed for clear harmonic speech may be
weakened. Sul ponticello can increase brilliance but may also destabilize the selected mode or introduce
competing high partials. Pizzicato harmonics are possible, especially as natural harmonics, but their
amplitude and decay differ markedly from bowed harmonics. Rimsky-Korsakov regarded pizzicato
harmonics as especially successful on cello, although that observation reflects historical orchestral
practice rather than a controlled comparison (Rimsky-Korsakov, 1912/1922). 
1.3 Analytical and theoretical literature: texture
Within texture, harmonics can perform at least four analytically distinguishable functions.
Spectral differentiation occurs when harmonic sonorities are separated from ordinary stopped-string
sound. Because the fundamental and low-order spectral distribution are reorganized, a harmonic layer
can become perceptually segregated even when its register and rhythm resemble those of adjacent
strings.
Timbral fusion occurs when harmonics approximate or merge with flutes, piccolos, high woodwinds,
glass-like percussion, or other sounds dominated by relatively sparse high-frequency components. The
historical orchestration literature repeatedly identifies a link between string harmonics and flute timbre.
The claim should be understood as an orchestral affinity rather than spectral identity (Rimsky-Korsakov,
1912/1922). 
Registral extension and de-corporealization occur when a string section is projected beyond its
ordinary stopped-register identity. The reduction or displacement of low-frequency energy can weaken
the perceptual association between sounding pitch and the physical scale of the instrument. A cello or
double bass may consequently contribute to a high, apparently source-ambiguous stratum.
Textural transformation occurs when ordinary sound is converted gradually into harmonic, harmonic-
glissando, half-harmonic, or multiphonic sound. Such processes change not merely tone colour but also
pitch definiteness, internal spectral multiplicity, attack clarity, and the perceived number of concurrent
components.
These functions can be described through the Taxonomy of Orchestral Grouping Effects. Increased
timbral similarity may facilitate fusion or sequential integration; increased difference may promote
segregation, stratification, or formal segmentation. Multiphonics are particularly ambiguous because
several pitch components may be heard as one complex source, as a fused chord, or as unstable
internal streams. Spectromorphological terminology is useful for describing the resulting spectral
trajectories and transformations, although Smalley’s framework was devised primarily for
electroacoustic listening and does not itself establish the instrumental production mechanism
(Goodchild & McAdams, 2021; McAdams et al., 2022; Smalley, 1997).

1.4 Treatises and representative repertoire
Harmonics were systematized in eighteenth-century violin pedagogy, notably in L’Abbé le fils’s Principes
du violon of 1761, where both natural and artificial harmonics were addressed. Their subsequent
development in virtuoso violin playing, particularly through Paganini and nineteenth-century
successors, transformed them from special effects into an extended melodic technique (Hann, 2015;
Stowell, 1978). 
Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century orchestration treatises commonly emphasized three uses:
sustained high sonorities, tremolando, and the mediation of string and woodwind colour . Modern
treatises place greater emphasis on precise fingering, alternative touch intervals, glissandi, and
extended harmonic techniques. This historical shift reflects both improved performance standards and
a conceptual movement from “colouristic exception” to parametrically controlled sound production.
Representative modern and contemporary applications include harmonic fields and glissandi in
Stravinsky’s orchestral writing; harmonics as a means of timbral and registral continuity in Ravel;
multiphonic-like string effects in Ligeti’s Apparitions; the systematic exploitation of natural-harmonic
tuning in Ligeti’s later works; and harmonic glissandi, multiphonics, and transitions between pitch and
noise in works by Xenakis, Scelsi, Lachenmann, Scodanibbio, and later performer-composers. In such
repertoire, harmonics often cease to function as ornamental high notes and become constitutive of a
texture’s spectral identity or transformation. Guettler and Thelin identify effects resembling later
multiphonic practice in Ligeti’s Apparitions, while Fallowfield documents the subsequent technical
stabilization of cello multiphonics (Fallowfield, 2020; Guettler & Thelin, 2012). 
2. Sul tasto and flautando
2.1 Acoustics and timbre
Sul tasto directs the bow away from the bridge and toward, or over , the fingerboard. This increases the
bow–bridge distance relative to the speaking length of the string. Under typical performance
conditions, the resulting tone contains less relative high-partial energy, a lower spectral centroid, a
softer attack, and a greater proportion of bow noise relative to strongly radiated upper harmonics. It is
commonly perceived as veiled, diffuse, soft-edged, or flute-like.
This result does not arise from position alone. Stable bowed-string motion permits different ranges of
bow force at different bowing points. As the bow is moved away from the bridge, the usable maximum
force falls; performers normally compensate through force, velocity, hair inclination, and contact area.
Schoonderwaldt’s experiments showed that bow force dominated measured spectral-centroid variation,
while bow–bridge distance and velocity conditioned the available force range. Sul tasto should therefore
be described as a coordinated bowing regime rather than as a geometrical location with an automatic
timbral output (Schoonderwaldt, 2009). 
The relative, rather than absolute, bow position is decisive. Bowing five centimetres from the bridge
does not have the same acoustic significance on an open cello C string, a stopped violin E-string note,
and a high artificial harmonic. The operative variable is the bowing distance as a proportion of the
current speaking length. As the left hand ascends, the string is shortened and the bowing point must
generally move toward the bridge to preserve a comparable ratio. Consequently, literal bowing “over
the fingerboard” may be acoustically or physically unsuitable in very high positions.

Flautando overlaps with sul tasto but should not automatically be treated as its synonym. Sul tasto
specifies or implies contact-point displacement. Flautando specifies a desired flute-like, low-resistance,
lightly articulated colour and is commonly realized through a combination of bow placement toward the
fingerboard, relatively light force, and freely moving bow speed. A passage can therefore be played sul
tasto without achieving a convincing flautando character , and a player may approximate flautando
without moving to the most extreme fingerboard position. Historical and contemporary sources are
inconsistent on this distinction, so the intended degree and character should be clarified when
compositionally important.
2.2 Notation and performance practice
The standard verbal indications include sul tasto, sulla tastiera, sur la touche, am Griffbrett, flautando, and
the abbreviation s.t. Return to the conventional sounding point may be indicated by ordinario, ord., 
normale, or nat. Because naturale can also concern harmonics, mute state, vibrato, or another preceding
technique, an explicit “normal bow position” indication is preferable in dense contemporary notation.
Degrees may be specified as poco sul tasto, sul tasto, molto sul tasto, or “as far over the fingerboard as
possible.” Gradual transitions are represented through arrows, dashed extension lines, verbal
instructions such as gradualmente al tasto, or graphic contact-point staves. A contemporary notation key
should state whether the endpoints describe literal locations or perceptual target colours. Sekulić’s
notation handbook emphasizes that gradual timbral changes require coordination of contact point,
pressure, bow division, and speed rather than simple lateral movement (Sekulić, n.d.). 
Most ordinary bowed articulations—legato, détaché, tremolo, trill, measured repetition, double stops,
and many harmonics—can be combined with sul tasto. The degree of successful combination is not
uniform. Rapid, lightly bowed tremolo may reinforce the diffuse colour , whereas forceful martelé and
accented attacks tend to counteract it. High natural or artificial harmonics can lose pitch clarity when
played too far from the bridge. Double stops may require different compromises because the two
strings and sounding lengths do not respond identically. Pizzicato cannot literally be sul tasto as a bow-
position technique, although plucking location may be independently specified.
Instrument-specific considerations
On violin and viola, sul tasto is rapidly accessible and can be sustained over broad melodic and tremolo
textures. Very high left-hand positions reduce the available space over the fingerboard and alter the
appropriate relative sounding point. The viola’s greater variation in body and string length makes
standardized absolute-position instructions particularly unreliable.
On cello, the long speaking length allows a broad perceptual gradient between ordinary and
fingerboard-oriented bowing. In high thumb positions, however , the left hand may occupy the same
physical region in which an extreme sul tasto bow would normally operate. Bow placement must then
be adjusted or interpreted perceptually rather than literally.
On double bass, the long strings permit a wide contact-point continuum, and sul tasto can produce a
strongly differentiated diffuse low-register spectrum. The effect is nevertheless conditioned by string
construction, mensur , bow type, tuning, and the relatively high proportion of mechanical and bow
noise. Very soft low-register sul tasto can lose pitch definition more readily than the equivalent violin
texture.

2.3 Analytical and theoretical literature: texture
The principal textural effect of sul tasto is usually not simple attenuation but spectral convergence. By
reducing salient upper partials and attack definition, it can decrease the perceptual separability of
individual instruments and voices. In homogeneous rhythmic writing, this can encourage section-level
fusion and produce a continuous background stratum. Where only one section or voice adopts the
technique, a contrast may instead be established between a diffuse layer and more sharply articulated
ordinary or sul ponticello material.
Within the auditory-grouping framework, sul tasto can contribute to:
blend, by reducing spectral distinctions among string instruments;
stratification, by placing a low-salience sustained layer behind brighter material;
surface-texture formation, when individual lines become difficult to track but collective activity
remains audible;
segmental articulation, when entry into or departure from sul tasto marks a boundary;
timbral augmentation, when a subdued string layer colours another instrumental source
without becoming separately prominent.
These are conditional analytical functions, not meanings intrinsic to the technique. A solo sul tasto line
in a sparse register may remain foregrounded, while a louder or rhythmically differentiated sul tasto
layer may resist fusion. Timbre operates together with onset synchrony, register , spatial placement,
dynamics, and voice leading (Goodchild & McAdams, 2021; McAdams et al., 2022). 
Gradual movement toward the fingerboard can also function as a process of spectral contraction. In
spectromorphological terms, the sound’s high-frequency occupation and attack profile may be
progressively reduced while pitch and rhythmic identity remain stable. Such a process can be used to
dissolve a foreground line into a textural field without changing its notated pitches.
2.4 Treatises and representative repertoire
Early discussions of varying bow contact point, including Ganassi’s sixteenth-century viol technique, do
not necessarily document the modern, explicitly differentiated categories of sul tasto and sul ponticello.
Later historical claims should therefore distinguish general advice about sounding point from a notated
special effect. Farina’s Capriccio stravagante of 1627 constitutes stronger evidence of deliberate
colouristic bow-position manipulation, although its imitative and theatrical aims differ from modern
parametric treatment (Bonner , 2018). 
By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, orchestration treatises presented sul tasto as a
recognized means of producing a veiled or flute-like string sonority. Rimsky-Korsakov paired sul tasto
and flautando and contrasted their “dull, veiled” quality with the metallic colour of sul ponticello. Such
language has remained influential but compresses a continuous acoustic field into two categorical
effects (Rimsky-Korsakov, 1912/1922). 
In twentieth-century quartet and orchestral repertory, sul tasto is frequently placed in direct opposition
to ordinary and sul ponticello bowing. Berg’s Lyric Suite, Bartók’s quartets, Janáček’s Intimate Letters,
Ligeti’s string writing, Crumb’s chamber music, and later works by Xenakis, Saariaho, and Lachenmann
use contact-point contrasts to separate layers, alter continuity, or transform an apparently stable pitch
configuration. In Janáček’s Intimate Letters, for example, simultaneous differentiated techniques among

3. Sul ponticello
3.1 Acoustics and timbre
Sul ponticello places the bow closer to the bridge than in ordinary playing. In its moderate form, upper
partials become more prominent and the tone is commonly perceived as bright, nasal, glassy, or
metallic. In more extreme forms, the fundamental may become perceptually weak, selected upper
harmonics may dominate, and the sound may acquire beating, squealing, intermittent, or noise-rich
components.
The mechanism should not be reduced to the claim that bowing near a node “removes the
fundamental.” A normally bowed string is a nonlinear stick–slip system. Moving the bow toward the
bridge narrows the range of force–velocity combinations that sustain stable Helmholtz motion. When
bow force is insufficient for that position, several slips can occur during one nominal period. Guettler
documented extreme sul ponticello signals containing multiple slips and a consequent emphasis on
corresponding upper harmonics. Thus, the characteristic spectrum can arise both from the excitation
geometry and from a changed motion regime (Guettler , 2002; Guettler , 2004). 
Bow force remains crucial. A player can bow near the bridge with sufficient force and coordinated
velocity while retaining a comparatively full, projecting tone; conversely, a deliberately low-force bridge-
adjacent stroke produces a more fragile and overtone-dominated result. The labels sul ponticello, molto
sul ponticello, and “on the bridge” therefore describe different and partly overlapping acoustic regimes.
Bowing directly on the bridge is a separate extended technique and should not be conflated with
ordinary sul ponticello.
The spectrum of sul ponticello is often temporally unstable. Slight movement of bow position changes
which partials are reinforced; irregular friction and torsional string motion add modulated noise; and
body resonances selectively radiate the resulting components. A single average spectral centroid
cannot adequately represent these changes. Spectral slope, harmonic-to-noise ratio, partial salience,
temporal modulation, attack irregularity, and frame-level variance are also required.
3.2 Notation and performance practice
Standard indications include sul ponticello, sul pont., s.p., au chevalet, and am Steg. Intensification may be
written as molto sul ponticello, estremamente sul ponticello, or “as close to the bridge as possible.” Return
is indicated by ordinario, normale, or an explicit “normal bow position.”
A distinction should be maintained among:
poco sul ponticello: a moderate brightening while retaining a stable pitch core;
sul ponticello: clearly bridge-oriented, overtone-rich playing;
molto sul ponticello: an extreme position with reduced fundamental stability;
on the bridge: bow hair contacting the bridge itself;
behind/sub ponticello: bowing the afterlength between bridge and tailpiece.

These techniques are physically and acoustically different. They should not be represented by an
undifferentiated abbreviation.
Gradual contact-point changes can be written by verbal arrows, extension lines, or a dedicated graphic
staff. Where timbral trajectory is structurally important, endpoint definitions and the expected relation
between movement and duration should be given. Because the performer must continually coordinate
force and speed, a graphic line specifying position alone does not fully determine the result.
Most bowed articulations combine readily with sul ponticello. Unmeasured tremolo is especially
common because it sustains an active, fluctuating upper spectrum while minimizing the need for long
stable strokes. Trills and finger tremolos can create dense modulation between pitch, partial, and
friction noise. Harmonics can be brightened or destabilized; a carefully controlled bridge-oriented bow
may support them, whereas extreme multiple-slip behaviour can obscure their pitch. Forceful double
stops are possible but demanding because both strings require viable friction conditions. Again,
pizzicato is not literally combinable as a bow-position technique, although bridge-adjacent plucking
constitutes a related but separate colour .
Instrument-specific considerations
The violin produces a highly exposed and rapidly changing high-partial spectrum. Small contact-point
movements have large proportional effects, especially in upper positions. This makes violin sul ponticello
agile but also sensitive to differences among performers.
On viola, the technique can counteract the instrument’s relatively darker ordinary spectrum and
produce marked separation from violin or cello layers. The result depends strongly on instrument
dimensions and string choice.
The cello offers a broad range from mild nasal coloration to complex, whistle-like or adjacent-harmonic
phenomena. Messina describes controllable transitions between very high partials as the bow is
displaced close to the bridge, although such control is virtuosic and instrument-dependent (Messina,
2009). 
On double bass, high partials may become clearly detached from the low nominal fundamental, and the
long string facilitates spatially differentiated excitation points. The result can possess considerable
projection despite the weakening of low-frequency pitch identity. Mechanical and friction noise are
correspondingly prominent, and section playing introduces substantial inter-player spectral dispersion.
3.3 Analytical and theoretical literature: texture
Sul ponticello is especially effective in creating timbral heterogeneity. When assigned to one layer
against ordinary or muted strings, it can prevent fusion even where rhythm, register , and pitch content
remain similar . Conversely, when applied to an entire section, it can establish a coherent collective
timbre whose internal fluctuations produce a dense surface texture.
Its principal textural functions include:
differentiation of simultaneous layers;
intensification of internal activity without increased note density;
conversion of pitch-defined sound into spectrally distributed or noise-adjacent material;
creation of foreground salience through high-frequency energy;

The distinction between event density and spectral activity is analytically important. A sustained sul
ponticello tone may have one notated onset but contain rapid fluctuations among partials, friction
events, and noise bands. In textural analysis it should not automatically be classified as internally static
merely because its pitch and notation are sustained.
The Taxonomy of Orchestral Grouping Effects explains how the increased timbral contrast of sul
ponticello can produce segregation or foregrounding, while a whole-section application can create an
integrated surface texture. Spectromorphology is useful for describing the transition from stable
harmonic spectrum toward fluctuating, granular , or turbulent behaviour , but the instrumental bowing
variables should remain analytically distinct from their perceptual consequences (McAdams et al., 2022;
Smalley, 1997). 
3.4 Treatises and representative repertoire
Farina’s Capriccio stravagante is frequently cited as an early documented example of bridge-oriented
bowing. The work’s instructions and imitative programme demonstrate deliberate exploitation of
unconventional string colour , although modern terminology should not be projected uncritically onto all
earlier sounding-point advice (Bonner , 2018). 
Later orchestration treatises typically associated sul ponticello with tremolo and metallic colour . Rimsky-
Korsakov’s account reflects its late-Romantic orchestral use but already recognizes bow position as a
radical modifier of section timbre. In his own Legend of the Invisible City of Kitezh, a solo violin is placed
over a harmonic background of sul ponticello strings and woodwinds, showing that the technique had
acquired a stratifying rather than merely ornamental function (Rimsky-Korsakov, 1912/1922). 
In the early twentieth century, sul ponticello was increasingly used as a structural contrast in chamber
music. It participates in extended timbral trajectories in works by Schoenberg, Bartók, Berg, and
Janáček. Penderecki’s early string works, including Threnody to the Victims of Hiroshima, place it within
large-scale fields of tremolo, clusters, glissandi, harmonics, approximate pitch, and behind-the-bridge
sound. The techniques collectively reorganize the orchestra into changing sound masses rather than
stable instrumental choirs.
Ligeti’s string writing similarly employs bow-position differentiation as one parameter within textures
characterized by internal transformation, superposition, and changing degrees of fusion. Lachenmann’s
Pression and subsequent works move beyond a binary opposition between normal and special timbre:
contact point, pressure, friction regime, and bodily gesture become components of the musical material
itself. Recent scholarship on Pression has attempted to classify these techniques according to their
production and perceptual characteristics, confirming the analytical inadequacy of treating them merely
as isolated “effects.”

4. Con sordino
4.1 Acoustics and timbre
A bridge-mounted string mute changes both loudness and timbre. Its primary physical action is to add
mass and, depending on construction and contact, damping to the bridge. The bridge’s mobility and its
coupling to the instrument body are consequently altered. High-frequency transmission is generally
reduced, resonant peaks are shifted or reshaped, and the radiated spectrum becomes less brilliant. The
mute should therefore be understood as a mechanical filter attached to the bridge rather than as a
device that simply “absorbs the overtones.”
Gough explains that the added mass lowers the bridge’s high-frequency filtering action, producing a
softer and less intense radiated sound. In violin measurements by Elie, Gautier , and David, mute
application produced relatively little change in the lowest measured region but reduced mean bridge
mobility by approximately 5–13 dB between about 1 and 2.5 kHz. This region includes a substantial part
of the violin’s projection and brilliance characteristics. The exact response depends on mute mass,
placement, bridge, instrument, string, and bowing (Elie et al., 2013; Gough, 2016). 
A standard orchestral mute and a heavy practice or hotel mute are not acoustically interchangeable. A
performance mute is intended principally to modify orchestral colour while preserving usable response,
projection, and intonation. A heavy practice mute is intended to achieve substantially greater sound-
level reduction. Evangelista and Freire’s 2025 study of heavy violin practice mutes used long-term
average spectra and loudness measures and found characteristic spectral changes that could not be
described merely as a stronger version of light muting. In particular , heavy mutes produced different
patterns of energy redistribution, including reduced energy transfer into lower-frequency regions in
some configurations. The mass, material, geometry, and bridge contact all contributed to the result
(Evangelista & Freire, 2025).

The category “mute” was historically heterogeneous. Loughridge shows that seventeenth- and
eighteenth-century materials, terminology, function, and perceived meaning were not standardized.
Metal remained common well into the eighteenth century, while commentators disagreed over whether
the principal function was reduced loudness, changed timbre, imitation, or affective characterization.
Modern synthetic performance mutes should therefore not automatically be treated as neutral
substitutes for historical devices (Loughridge, 2016). 
4.2 Notation and performance practice
The standard instructions are con sordino or con sord. for application and senza sordino, senza sord., or 
via sordino for removal. French and German equivalents include avec sourdine/sans sourdine and mit
Dämpfer/ohne Dämpfer. The mute remains in use until an explicit removal instruction is given.
If a heavy practice mute, metal mute, historical mute, adjustable mute, or partial muting device is
required, the type must be named. The unqualified con sordino instruction will normally be interpreted
as a standard performance mute. A pictogram or verbal specification should be included in both score
and parts for an unfamiliar device.
Time must be allowed for application and removal. Mutes that remain attached to the strings below the
bridge can often be engaged quickly, but loose, rigid, heavy, historical, or bass mutes may require
appreciably more time and may produce incidental handling noise. The required interval is therefore
device- and instrument-specific rather than a universal number of seconds. The orchestration should
also provide acoustic cover if a completely silent change is expected (Goss, 2012). 
The mute can be combined with ordinary bowed articulations, tremolo, trills, glissandi, harmonics, sul
tasto, and sul ponticello. These combinations are not acoustically additive. A mute may reduce the high-
frequency distinction expected from sul ponticello, although sufficiently extreme bridge bowing can
restore substantial upper-partial activity. Muted harmonics may lose projection because both the string

excitation and bridge transfer reduce lower-energy components. Muted sul tasto can produce a very
low-salience layer but may also become pitch-indistinct. The combined result should therefore be tested
rather than inferred by summing verbal timbre labels.
Instrument-specific considerations
The same bridge-mass principle applies to violin, viola, cello, and double bass, but the evidence base is
heavily violin-centred. Bridge mass, bridge dimensions, body modes, string impedance, and radiating
efficiency differ substantially among the four instruments. It cannot be assumed that a frequency-
specific violin result—for example, attenuation between 1 and 2.5 kHz—will transfer unchanged to cello
or double bass.
Violin and viola performance mutes are generally light and quickly accessible. On cello, the larger
bridge and lower modal distribution produce a distinct muted response, often retaining considerable
low-register body while reducing projection and upper-frequency definition. Double-bass mutes are
larger , and both bridge and setup vary extensively; physical handling and sound-level consequences
may therefore differ among instruments and orchestras. Controlled comparative data across all four
remain absent.
4.3 Analytical and theoretical literature: texture
Con sordino frequently promotes blend by reducing high-frequency cues that distinguish individual
sources. A muted string section can consequently be heard as a more homogeneous spectral field. This
does not imply that all muted instruments fuse automatically: differences of register , onset, articulation,
spatial position, and pitch trajectory can preserve separate streams.
In orchestral texture the technique may serve:
to lower the perceptual salience of an accompaniment;
to differentiate foreground and background without changing pitch or rhythm;
to transform a conventional string choir into a source-ambiguous colour;
to soften attacks and reduce local segmentation;
to unify divided parts into a spectral layer;
to create a distinct formal region;
to establish contrast between muted and unmuted subsections;
to mediate between strings and other subdued instrumental families.
A heavy practice mute can produce a more radical change. Because instrumental response and
performer feedback are substantially altered, it may affect articulation, bow compensation, dynamic
scaling, and perceived distance. In Berio’s Sequenza VIII, the replacement of the ordinary mute by a
practice mute near the end is therefore not merely a further dynamic reduction. It creates a different
physical and spectral condition and contributes to the work’s progressive redefinition of violin gesture
and resonance (Berio, 1976; Haffner , 2016). 
The historical meanings of muted colour should not be universalized. Loughridge demonstrates that
eighteenth-century muted violins could signify murmuring water , grief, sleep, magic, distance, or
practical softness, depending on context. The later critical vocabulary of the “ethereal” or “otherworldly”
represents only part of that history (Loughridge, 2016).

4.4 Treatises and representative repertoire
Secure evidence for purpose-made violin mutes emerges around the later seventeenth century,
although earlier descriptions of placing objects on the bridge do not necessarily document
standardized musical practice. Loughridge identifies approximately 1680 as a point at which the violin
mute can be established with greater confidence as both a named device and a material object. Lully,
Purcell, Charpentier , Vivaldi, and later Haydn belong to a repertory in which terminology and materials
remained variable. Vivaldi’s con piombi directions are particularly significant, but their exact physical
realization remains unresolved (Loughridge, 2016). 
By the nineteenth century, muting was established as an orchestral colour in instrumentation treatises.
Berlioz described small wooden devices, while later authors increasingly associated muted strings with
distance, mystery, subdued expression, or altered projection. Rimsky-Korsakov characterized muted
strings as reduced in volume, dull in soft dynamics, and potentially hiss-like in loud dynamics. That
description remains useful as a historical observation but overstates uniformity across instruments,
mute types, and performance conditions (Rimsky-Korsakov, 1912/1922). 
Important repertorial contexts include muted slow movements and scene-painting in eighteenth-
century opera and symphony; Berlioz, Wagner , and later Romantic orchestration; extended muted
passages and rapid mute-state contrasts in Debussy, Ravel, Bartók, Berg, and Shostakovich; and the
explicit use of non-standard mutes in later twentieth-century music. Berio’s Sequenza VIII is a
particularly clear instance in which a heavy practice mute is compositionally differentiated from an
ordinary performance mute.

characteristics, loudness, and inter-player variability. For harmonics and multiphonics, the number and
salience of perceptually distinct pitch components should also be represented. For mutes,
measurements should distinguish absolute attenuation from redistribution of spectral energy.
6. Open questions and gaps
6.1 Lack of cross-instrument acoustic datasets
Most controlled studies concern violin. Comparable measurements of viola, cello, and double bass
under standardized sul tasto, sul ponticello, harmonic, and mute conditions are lacking. A systematic
dataset should control pitch, written and sounding register , string, dynamic, bow force, velocity, contact-
point ratio, articulation, mute mass, and recording geometry.
6.2 Insufficient modelling of technique continua
Published datasets frequently classify samples into discrete labels such as ordinario, sul tasto, and sul
ponticello. The underlying variables are continuous. Research is required on how measured acoustic
descriptors evolve along controlled contact-point trajectories and whether perceptual category
boundaries correspond to physical thresholds.
6.3 Underdeveloped mute research
The 2025 study by Evangelista and Freire substantially improves the evidence on heavy violin practice
mutes, but the literature remains sparse. No consensual classification links mute mass, material,
geometry, contact area, bridge mobility, radiated spectrum, loudness, and performer feedback across
the violin family. Viola, cello, and double-bass data are especially deficient. 
6.4 Lack of notation standardization
Natural-harmonic notation remains ambiguous between touched and sounding pitch. Double-bass
transposition introduces further uncertainty. Half harmonic, multiphonic, degree of sul tasto or sul
ponticello, and non-standard mute indications lack universally accepted conventions. Explanatory
legends and performer consultation remain necessary.

6.5 Multiphonic reproducibility
The physical basis of bowed-string multiphonics has been established, but instrument-to-instrument
and performer-to-performer reproducibility remains incompletely characterized. Relevant variables
include scale length, string construction, torsional response, finger compliance, bow hair , rosin, body
radiation, and listening threshold. The cello and double bass are better documented than violin and
viola (Fallowfield, 2020; Guettler & Thelin, 2012). 
6.6 Ensemble and section-level behaviour
Most measurements use isolated solo notes. Orchestral texture involves multiple players whose bow
positions, partial amplitudes, intonation, and transients are not identical. Such dispersion may either
broaden a fused spectral mass or increase perceptual heterogeneity. The relation between solo-
instrument measurements and section-level texture has not been adequately quantified.
6.7 Technique recognition remains incomplete
Machine-listening research confirms that playing-technique identification is substantially harder than
ordinary instrument recognition. In the Studio On Line dataset, a system incorporating time-frequency
scattering and metric learning achieved substantially lower performance for technique recognition than
for instrument recognition. This difference reflects the large within-class variability of instrumental
techniques and the acoustic overlap among nominal categories (Lostanlen et al., 2018). 
6.8 Texture-specific analytical literature remains thin
A considerable amount has been written on how to produce and notate these techniques, but
comparatively little scholarship directly analyses their role in constructing musical texture across a
representative corpus. The relationship between technique, spectral result, auditory grouping, and
formal function is frequently asserted rather than empirically tested. Existing orchestration and timbre
frameworks provide a strong conceptual basis, but targeted score, performance, and listening studies
remain necessary.
